## Conceptual Framework

This map is not intended as a closed classification, but as a practical aid for designing more coherent, varied and useful assessment in the classroom. In educational assessment, it is useful to distinguish between what students do or produce, how that information is gathered, which criteria are used to interpret it and which teaching decision is made from it.

**Techniques** are procedures for gathering, analysing or contrasting evidence of learning. **Cross-cutting dimensions** situate assessment pedagogically according to the agent, purpose, timing, curricular approach, type of evidence, medium and methodological context. **Assessable evidence** refers to the tasks, activities, products, performances or responses through which students make their learning visible. **Assessment instruments** make it possible to record, value, provide feedback on, grade or communicate that evidence, although not every instrument has to be used for grading.

The pedagogical usefulness of the map depends on how teachers use it: evidence should be aligned with assessment criteria, sufficient and varied, and should take into account the real classroom context, student diversity and a manageable workload. The same activity can serve different functions depending on timing and purpose: diagnostic, formative, summative, competency-based, inclusive or accrediting.

Therefore, the relationships between techniques, dimensions, evidence and instruments should be understood as guidance for decision-making, not as automatic recipes. Good assessment means selecting relevant evidence, interpreting it with clear criteria, providing useful feedback and, when appropriate, translating it into a justifiable grade.

## Assessment Techniques

### 1. Systematic observation

_Planned and documented observation of performance during an activity._

**Phase:** Process · **Participation:** Teacher

As a technique in the strict sense, this element describes a procedure for gathering evidence. It can be combined with different purposes, agents and methodological contexts.

Systematic observation is a technique in which the teacher plans in advance what to observe, at which moments and with which recording instrument. It is not a matter of looking in a generic way, but of attending to specific, observable criteria while students are performing.

How to apply it: before the session, the indicators to be observed are defined (e.g. 'uses laboratory materials correctly', 'argues with evidence', 'listens actively'). During the activity, the teacher uses a checklist, observation scale or rubric to record what they see. At the end, this information is used to give feedback, adjust teaching or contribute to grading.

It is especially useful in practical activities, debates, oral presentations, workshops and cooperative work, where the evidence of learning occurs in real time and is not captured in a physical product.

**Purpose:** Record actions, procedures, participation and habits.

**When to use:** When evidence occurs in real time and reviewing a final product is not enough.

**Typical evidence:** Oral performances, practical work, group work, participation.

**Limitations:** Can be subjective if there are no clear criteria.

**Associated media/evidence:** Debate, Oral presentation, Laboratory practice, Workshop, Cooperative work, Simulation, Individual classroom work, Class participation, Assembly, Drama, Prototype, Field notebook

**Related assessment instruments:** Checklist, Observation scale, Analytic rubric, Anecdotal record

### 2. Incidental observation

_Recording of relevant events that arise unexpectedly._

**Phase:** Process · **Participation:** Teacher

As a technique in the strict sense, this element describes a procedure for gathering evidence. It can be combined with different purposes, agents and methodological contexts.

Incidental observation records relevant events that emerge spontaneously during everyday classroom activity, without the teacher having anticipated them. A perceptive comment, a recurring difficulty, a group conflict or an unexpected initiative are all examples of incidental evidence.

How to apply it: the teacher should always have an anecdotal record, a notebook or an application to hand in which to briefly note the date, the student's name and the observed event. These notes, accumulated over time, reveal patterns of behaviour, progress or difficulties that planned observation might not capture.

It does not replace systematic observation, but complements it by providing unexpected evidence and qualitative insights into the learning process.

**Purpose:** Detect progress, difficulties or significant incidents.

**When to use:** When an unforeseen but relevant piece of evidence appears.

**Typical evidence:** Behaviours, comments, conflicts, initiatives, doubts.

**Limitations:** Does not replace systematic observation.

**Associated media/evidence:** Individual classroom work, Class participation, Cooperative work

**Related assessment instruments:** Anecdotal record, Field notes, Teacher's diary

---


### 3. Analysis of productions

_Assessment of products created by students._

**Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher

As a technique in the strict sense, this element describes a procedure for gathering evidence. It can be combined with different purposes, agents and methodological contexts.

The analysis of productions consists in assessing the artefacts or products created by students: written texts, reports, projects, presentations, videos, concept maps, scale models, portfolios or any other tangible or digital product.

How to apply it: the criteria to be assessed and the instrument to be used (rubric, correction guide, descriptive scale) are defined in advance. The review can be done outside class time, which allows the teacher to dedicate more time and attention than during live observation. It is essential to return written feedback that explains not only the level achieved, but what can be improved and how.

When the final product does not reflect the full learning (because the process was complex or the work was done in a group), it is advisable to complement it with process monitoring, drafts or brief interviews.

**Purpose:** Assess quality, content, structure, process and transfer.

**When to use:** When there is an assessable product or artefact.

**Typical evidence:** Texts, reports, projects, videos, maps, infographics, portfolios.

**Limitations:** Does not show the full process if not accompanied by ongoing monitoring.

**Associated media/evidence:** Class notebook, Laboratory report, Project defence, Essay, Infographic, Video, Podcast, Scale model, Learning portfolio, Research report, Monograph, Text commentary, Graph or data commentary, Summary, Outline, Concept map, Mind map, Timeline, Glossary, Reading card, Review, Popular science article, Project report, Case study, Document-based test, Academic poster, Digital presentation, Video tutorial, Comic, Digital model, Prototype, Website, Blog post, Collaborative document, Digital portfolio, Initial concept map, Draft, Partial submission, Field notebook, Educational micro-video

**Related assessment instruments:** Analytic rubric, Correction guide, Descriptive scale, Feedback card

### 4. Specific tests

_Situations specifically designed to check learning._

**Phase:** Initial/Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher

As a technique in the strict sense, this element describes a procedure for gathering evidence. It can be combined with different purposes, agents and methodological contexts.

Specific tests are situations specifically designed to check learning under controlled conditions. They include written development tests, multiple choice tests, short answer tests, oral tests and practical tests.

How to apply them: the design must be aligned with the assessment criteria and the knowledge covered. Each question or task should be linked to a specific criterion. It is recommended to share the correction criteria in advance (or at least the type of task) so that students can prepare in an oriented way.

They are useful for accrediting achievements and comparing results, but should not be the only assessment instrument. Combined with other evidence (productions, observation, self-assessment), they offer a more complete and fair picture of learning.

**Purpose:** Check knowledge, application, reasoning or skills.

**When to use:** When a specific or comparable check is needed.

**Typical evidence:** Written, oral or practical responses under controlled conditions.

**Limitations:** Can reduce assessment if used as the only evidence.

**Associated media/evidence:** Written development test, Multiple-choice test, Oral test, Practical test, Digital questionnaire, Text commentary, Problem solving, Case study, Objective test, Short-answer test, Contextualised competency test, Document-based test

**Related assessment instruments:** Correction guide, Scoring scale, Correction template, Analytic rubric, Numerical scale

---


### 5. Oral exchanges

_Gathering evidence through oral communication._

**Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

As a technique in the strict sense, this element describes a procedure for gathering evidence. It can be combined with different purposes, agents and methodological contexts.

Oral exchanges are situations in which the teacher gathers learning evidence through oral communication: questions to the group, guided debates, colloquia, individual interviews or project defences.

How to apply them: it is advisable to plan the questions or script before the session and have a recording instrument ready (oral rubric, observation scale, checklist). If the exchange is a group activity, it is difficult to observe everyone at once; the focus of attention can be alternated or the session recorded for later review (with appropriate permissions).

They allow for the assessment of deep comprehension, argumentation, synthesis ability, fluency and oral expression — aspects that a written test does not capture. They also allow the teacher to ask follow-up questions to explore underlying reasoning.

**Purpose:** Assess comprehension, argumentation, expression and dialogue.

**When to use:** When assessing communication, verbal reasoning or interaction.

**Typical evidence:** Contributions, responses, debates, defences, interviews.

**Limitations:** Can depend on emotional factors.

**Associated media/evidence:** Debate, Colloquium, Interview, Oral presentation, Project defence, Assembly, Role play, Structured oral question, Oral test, Digital presentation, Podcast, Discussion forum, Brainstorming, Educational micro-video

**Related assessment instruments:** Oral rubric, Observation scale, Checklist, Anecdotal record

---


### 6. Probing and questioning

_Quick collection of responses, prior ideas or comprehension through brief questions._

**Phase:** Initial/Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student

As a technique in the strict sense, probing and questioning consists of gathering evidence through brief questions, oral or written, designed to make visible what students know, believe they know, do not understand or have just learned.

How to apply it: the teacher poses a question, a KPSI, an exit ticket, a brainstorming activity or a brief questionnaire. Responses are recorded with a simple instrument and analysed to decide whether to move forward, review, regroup, clarify or provide feedback.

It is especially useful in diagnostic and formative assessment, because it provides quick, actionable information. It should not be confused with a long test nor used only for grading.

**Purpose:** Explore prior knowledge, check immediate comprehension and detect adjustment needs.

**When to use:** When quick information is needed to start, adjust or close a session without turning the activity into an extensive test.

**Typical evidence:** Brief responses, oral questions, initial questionnaires, KPSI, exit tickets, brainstorming.

**Limitations:** Can yield superficial evidence if questions are not well formulated or are not used afterwards to make teaching decisions.

**Associated media/evidence:** KPSI, Initial concept map, Brainstorming, Exit ticket, Structured oral question, Digital questionnaire, Short-answer test, Multiple-choice test, Learning traffic light

**Related assessment instruments:** Digital form, Self-assessment scale, Checklist, Descriptive record, Individual tracking sheet

---


### 7. Analysis of processes and reflections

_Review of intermediate evidence, process records and student reflections._

**Phase:** Process · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

As a technique in the strict sense, the analysis of processes and reflections consists of reviewing evidence that shows how learning has developed: planning, drafts, decisions, difficulties, revisions, comments received and improvement commitments.

How to apply it: collection moments are selected during the task, and journals, logbooks, partial submissions, reflection cards or peer reviews are reviewed using a process rubric, scale or feedback card.

It is especially useful in projects, portfolios, cooperative work and complex tasks, because it allows assessment of the learning journey and guides improvement before the final submission.

**Purpose:** Understand how students learn, plan, revise, cooperate and improve during a task.

**When to use:** When it is important to assess learning during the process and not only the final product.

**Typical evidence:** Journals, logbooks, drafts, partial submissions, reflection cards, self-reports, peer reviews.

**Limitations:** Requires ongoing monitoring and clear criteria to avoid becoming a mere accumulation of documents.

**Associated media/evidence:** Learning diary, Reflection sheet, Self-report, Draft, Partial submission, Project log, Peer review, Two stars and a wish, Learning traffic light, Work plan, Meeting minutes, Learning contract, Collaborative document, Learning portfolio, Digital portfolio, AI process log

**Related assessment instruments:** Process rubric, Feedback card, Individual tracking sheet, Improvement commitments record, Self-assessment scale, Revision history, Progress dashboard, AI and sources use declaration

---


### 8. Evaluative interview

_Planned conversation to explore comprehension, reasoning or process._

**Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student

The evaluative interview is a conversation with an assessment purpose, prepared with questions or observation focuses. It allows exploration of how the student reasons, what decisions they made and what understanding underpins a production.

How to apply it: a brief script is defined, responses are recorded with a guide or oral rubric and questions are kept comparable across students. It can be used in project defences, portfolios or review of complex work.

It is useful for obtaining deep evidence, but must be planned to avoid excessive improvisation or unfair differences between interviews.

**Purpose:** Delve into evidence that is not clear from a product or test.

**When to use:** When it is necessary to probe student reasoning or verify authorship and comprehension.

**Typical evidence:** Oral responses, explanations, arguments, process decisions.

**Limitations:** Time-consuming and requires a common script to ensure fairness.

**Associated media/evidence:** Interview, Project defence, Digital portfolio

**Related assessment instruments:** Correction guide, Oral rubric, Anecdotal record, Observation guide

---


### 9. Survey and questionnaire

_Structured collection of responses through closed or open questions._

**Phase:** Initial/Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student

Surveys and questionnaires collect responses in a structured way. They can be used for diagnostic, formative or summative purposes depending on the design of the questions.

How to apply them: clear items are drafted, it is decided whether responses will be anonymous or identified, and results are analysed to make teaching decisions. In assessment, it is important to distinguish between perception questionnaires and knowledge tests.

They are useful for their speed and comparability, but should not replace richer evidence when complex learning is being assessed.

**Purpose:** Obtain quick information about knowledge, perceptions, interests or monitoring.

**When to use:** When comparable and easy-to-analyse information is needed from the whole group.

**Typical evidence:** Questionnaires, forms, initial surveys, perception scales.

**Limitations:** Quality depends greatly on question formulation.

**Associated media/evidence:** Digital questionnaire, KPSI, Exit ticket

**Related assessment instruments:** Digital form, Self-assessment scale, Correction template

---


### 10. Documentary analysis

_Review of documents, records and accumulated evidence._

**Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher

Documentary analysis consists of reviewing documents and records generated during learning: notebooks, portfolios, minutes, edit histories or evidence repositories.

How to apply it: relevant documents are selected, contrasted with criteria or indicators, and care is taken to avoid assessing only the quantity of accumulated material. The evidence map helps identify which document supports each criterion.

It is a useful technique for continuous and criteria-based assessment, especially when the process leaves verifiable traces.

**Purpose:** Interpret already-produced information to assess progress, coherence or traceability.

**When to use:** When learning is documented in accumulated records or products.

**Typical evidence:** Notebooks, portfolios, minutes, revision histories, evidence banks.

**Limitations:** Can overvalue what is documented and leave out unrecorded actions.

**Associated media/evidence:** Class notebook, Digital portfolio, Meeting minutes, Collaborative document, AI process log

**Related assessment instruments:** Evidence map by criterion, Revision history, Digital evidence bank, AI and sources use declaration

---


### 11. Analysis of digital interactions

_Interpretation of participation, traces and collaboration in digital environments._

**Phase:** Process · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

The analysis of digital interactions studies how students participate in digital spaces: contributions to shared documents, forums, comments, submissions, reviews and activity on platforms.

How to apply it: it is decided which traces are relevant to the criteria, histories or dashboards are reviewed and results are triangulated with products and teacher observation. Not all digital activity implies learning, so it must be interpreted with caution.

It is especially useful in collaborative projects and digital portfolios.

**Purpose:** Assess participation, contribution and process on platforms or shared documents.

**When to use:** When work is carried out in digital environments that leave useful traces.

**Typical evidence:** Forums, comments, edit histories, analytics, messages and digital submissions.

**Limitations:** Digital traces require pedagogical interpretation and respect for privacy.

**Associated media/evidence:** Discussion forum, Collaborative document, Digital notebook, Digital portfolio, AI process log

**Related assessment instruments:** Revision history, Learning analytics, Progress dashboard

---


### 12. Evidence triangulation

_Contrasting several pieces of evidence to make a more robust assessment decision._

**Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher

Evidence triangulation consists of contrasting several sources before making a decision: observation, production, test, interview, self-assessment or process record.

How to apply it: identify which criterion is to be assessed, what evidence demonstrates it and whether the evidence is sufficient and consistent with each other. If evidence is inconsistent, analyse which is more valid or whether it is worth gathering additional evidence.

It is key in competency-based and criteria-based assessment, because it avoids making decisions based on a single isolated task.

**Purpose:** Increase validity and reliability by combining products, observations, tests and reflections.

**When to use:** When a single piece of evidence is insufficient to assess a criterion or competency.

**Typical evidence:** Rubrics, records, tests, portfolios, interviews, self-assessments.

**Limitations:** Requires deciding which evidence carries more weight and avoiding duplication of the same evidence.

**Associated media/evidence:** Integrated competency task, E-portfolio with selected evidence, Contextualised competency test, AI process log

**Related assessment instruments:** Evidence map by criterion, Criteria grading table, Achievement profile, AI and sources use declaration

## Cross-cutting Dimensions

### 1. Self-assessment

_Students' assessment of their own learning._

**Category:** Assessment agent · **Phase:** Initial/Process/Final · **Participation:** Student

This dimension indicates who participates in the assessment of evidence. It does not determine the instrument on its own, but rather the role of the teacher, the students or peers in the assessment process.

Self-assessment is the process by which students assess their own learning, their work process or the result they have produced, using previously established and understood criteria.

How to apply it: it is not about asking for a free opinion ('did you enjoy it?'), but about providing a scale, rubric or list of specific indicators and asking students to apply them honestly to their own performance. It is advisable that they have previously seen examples of how those criteria are applied and discussed what each level means.

Its main value is metacognitive: it requires students to reflect on what they know, what they find difficult and how they can improve. When combined with the teacher's assessment, it allows discrepancies to be identified and self-regulation to be developed.

**Pedagogical function:** Foster metacognition, autonomy and self-regulation.

**When to use:** When students are expected to become aware of their own progress.

**Typical evidence:** Reflections, scales, journals, self-reports, KPSI.

**Precautions:** Requires training to avoid superficial responses.

**Associated media/evidence:** Learning diary, Learning portfolio, Learning contract, Reflection sheet, Self-report, KPSI, Learning traffic light, Exit ticket, Laboratory checklist

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Self-assessment scale, Assessment target, Self-assessment rubric, Checklist, Assessment traffic light, Self-assessment checklist, Improvement commitments record

---


### 2. Peer assessment

_Assessment of peers' performance or product using shared criteria._

**Category:** Assessment agent · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Peers

This dimension indicates who participates in the assessment of evidence. It does not determine the instrument on its own, but rather the role of the teacher, the students or peers in the assessment process.

Peer assessment is the assessment students make of the work or performance of their classmates, using shared criteria. It is a form of assessment among peers that, when well designed, improves understanding of criteria and develops critical thinking.

How to apply it: criteria must be clear, specific and have been worked on beforehand. It is recommended to start with tasks where assessing others' work is relatively straightforward (e.g. checking whether an outline includes the required elements) before moving on to more complex assessments. To reduce friendship bias, it can be done anonymously or in a cross-assessment format.

Its main function is formative: peer comments, combined with clear criteria, help to improve work before the final submission. It should only contribute to grading when students have sufficient practice and confidence in the process.

**Pedagogical function:** Improve work, understand criteria and learn among peers.

**When to use:** When introducing peer assessment and draft improvement.

**Typical evidence:** Comments, reviews, scales, rubrics between peers.

**Precautions:** Can be unreliable without clear criteria.

**Associated media/evidence:** Cooperative work, Debate, Assembly, Oral presentation, Meeting minutes, Collaborative document, Peer review, Two stars and a wish

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Peer assessment rubric, Observation scale, Digital form, Oral rubric, Cooperative work rubric, Cooperative work record, Peer assessment card

---


### 3. Teacher assessment

_Assessment carried out by the teacher or another external agent._

**Category:** Assessment agent · **Phase:** Initial/Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher

This dimension indicates who participates in the assessment of evidence. It does not determine the instrument on its own, but rather the role of the teacher, the students or peers in the assessment process.

Teacher assessment is the assessment carried out by the teacher or another external agent (an expert, a family member, another teacher). It is the most common form of assessment in the school system.

How to apply it: for it to be fair and transparent, it must be based on explicit criteria known by students in advance. The use of rubrics, correction guides or descriptive scales reduces subjectivity and facilitates consistency across different corrections. The feedback accompanying the assessment is just as important as the grade.

It should not be the only form of assessment: combining it with self-assessment and peer assessment offers a more complete picture of learning and fosters student autonomy and responsibility.

**Pedagogical function:** Assess learning using professional or institutional criteria.

**When to use:** When teacher assessment or grading is required.

**Typical evidence:** Any evidence gathered by the teacher.

**Precautions:** Should not exclude self-assessment and peer assessment.

**Associated media/evidence:** Laboratory practice, Oral presentation, Project defence, Learning portfolio, Laboratory report, Research report, Monograph, Essay, Written development test, Objective test, Multiple-choice test, Short-answer test, Oral test, Practical test, Contextualised competency test, Document-based test, Cooperative work, Open-book test

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Analytic rubric, Correction guide, Checklist, Observation scale, Grade book, Assessment record

---


### 4. Diagnostic purpose

_Gathering information about the starting point._

**Category:** Purpose and timing · **Phase:** Initial · **Participation:** Teacher/Student

This dimension clarifies what information is being gathered for and at which moment of learning it makes most sense. It helps prevent a diagnostic, formative or final activity from being used inappropriately.

Diagnostic assessment gathers information about students' starting point before beginning a unit, project or learning situation. Its aim is to detect prior knowledge, misconceptions, interests and needs, in order to adjust planning.

How to apply it: it can be done through open questions, KPSI (I know it / I think I know it / I don't know it), brainstorming, an initial concept map, a brief questionnaire or an exploratory task. What matters is that it is quick, non-penalising and that the results genuinely influence how teaching is subsequently designed.

A good diagnostic assessment allows the teacher to start from where students actually are, not from where the curriculum says they should be, and to design activities with greater precision.

**Pedagogical function:** Detect prior knowledge, interests and needs.

**When to use:** Before starting a unit, project or learning situation.

**Typical evidence:** Prior ideas, initial responses, maps, questionnaires.

**Precautions:** Must not be used for penalisation.

**Associated media/evidence:** Concept map, Objective test, Multiple-choice test, Short-answer test, KPSI, Initial concept map, Brainstorming

---


### 5. Formative purpose

_Assessment during the process to improve learning._

**Category:** Purpose and use · **Phase:** Process · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

This dimension focuses on the subsequent use of information: improving learning, accrediting achievements or making grading decisions.

Formative assessment is that whose main function is to improve learning while it is in progress, not to grade at the end. It is not a type of instrument, but a function that any assessment activity can fulfil if the information it generates is used to adjust teaching or learning.

How to apply it: it involves three steps: gather evidence (observation, questions, reviewing drafts, exit tickets), analyse it to identify where each student stands in relation to the criteria, and return useful and timely feedback so that students can act on it. The key is that the feedback arrives when there is still room to improve.

Formative assessment requires time and planning, but it is what has the greatest impact on learning according to educational research.

**Pedagogical function:** Regulate teaching and learning through feedback.

**When to use:** During learning, before finalising grading.

**Typical evidence:** Drafts, observations, reviews, questions, journals.

**Precautions:** Requires time to return useful information.

**Associated media/evidence:** Individual classroom work, Class participation, Colloquium, Assembly, Interview, Structured oral question, Class notebook, Learning diary, Learning portfolio, Summary, Outline, Mind map, Glossary, Reading card, Work plan, Learning contract, Reflection sheet, Short-answer test, Collaborative document, Brainstorming, Draft, Partial submission, Project log, Peer review, Two stars and a wish, Learning traffic light, Exit ticket, Cooperative work, Socratic seminar, Laboratory checklist, Field notebook, AI process log

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Feedback card, Checklist, Observation scale, Rating scale, Descriptive record, Teacher's diary, Group tracking sheet, Participation record, Field notes, Assessment card, Assessment traffic light, Improvement commitments record, Verbal scale, Revision history, Progress dashboard, Learning analytics, Digital evidence bank, Progress scale, Observation guide, Feedback/feedforward template, Group contract, Sociogram

---


### 6. Summative purpose

_Assessment of the degree of achievement at the end of a process._

**Category:** Purpose and use · **Phase:** Final · **Participation:** Teacher

This dimension focuses on the subsequent use of information: improving learning, accrediting achievements or making grading decisions.

Summative assessment evaluates the degree of achievement reached at the end of a learning period (unit, term, project, course). Its function is to accredit, certify or summarise what has been learned and is usually expressed as a grade.

How to apply it: it must be based on varied evidence gathered during the process, not only on a final test. Ideally, the final grade integrates information from different moments and formats (productions, performances, tests, self-assessment). The use of explicit criteria, known in advance, makes grading fairer and more understandable for students.

When combined with formative assessment throughout the process, the final grade better reflects actual learning and reduces the disproportionate weight of a single test.

**Pedagogical function:** Grade, accredit or summarise learning achieved.

**When to use:** At the end of a unit, term, project or course.

**Typical evidence:** Final products, tests, presentations, final portfolios.

**Precautions:** Should not be the only source of information.

**Associated media/evidence:** Oral presentation, Monograph, Essay, Text commentary, Review, Popular science article, Self-report, Written development test, Objective test, Multiple-choice test, Oral test, Document-based test

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Analytic rubric, Correction guide, Scoring scale, Grade book, Global or holistic rubric, Product rubric, Oral rubric, Rating scale, Correction template, Assessment card, Assessment matrix, Criteria grading table, Weighting matrix, Numerical scale, Verbal scale, Level-to-grade converter, Assessment spreadsheet, Individual assessment report, Assessment record, Digital rubric

### 7. Competency-based approach

_Assessment of the integrated mobilisation of knowledge in meaningful contexts._

**Category:** Curricular approach · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

This dimension expresses a broad curricular orientation. It affects the selection of evidence, criteria and instruments, but does not equate to a specific collection technique.

Competency-based assessment evaluates students' ability to mobilise knowledge, skills and attitudes in an integrated way in meaningful and contextualised situations. It is not limited to checking whether isolated content is known, but whether it can be applied in a real or plausible context.

How to apply it: it requires designing authentic tasks that resemble real-world or disciplinary situations. Assessment criteria must be linked to key competencies and the operational descriptors of the educational level. Competency rubrics and achievement profiles are particularly suitable tools.

Competency-based assessment involves assessing several areas of knowledge and competencies in an integrated way, which is a design challenge but offers a more faithful picture of what students are capable of doing.

**Pedagogical function:** Assess application, transfer and integration.

**When to use:** When assessing specific competencies and assessment criteria.

**Typical evidence:** Contextualised tasks, projects, cases, open-ended problems.

**Precautions:** Requires good contexts and observable criteria.

**Associated media/evidence:** Essay, Text commentary, Timeline, Review, Popular science article, Case study, Contextualised competency test, Document-based test, Infographic, Academic poster, Comic, Open-book test, Integrated competency task, Problem-situation, Field notebook, Educational micro-video, AI process log

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Competency rubric, Achievement profile, Assessment matrix, Descriptor bank, Criteria grading table, List of indicators per criterion, Evidence map by criterion

---


### 8. Performance evidence

_Assessment of how students perform in a complex or practical task._

**Category:** Type of evidence · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher

This dimension describes what kind of learning is made visible: performance, product, reasoning, process or interaction.

Performance-based assessment evaluates how students act in a complex or practical task: what decisions they make, how they organise the process, what procedures they apply and how they manage difficulties. The evidence is the observable performance, not just the result.

How to apply it: the teacher observes while students carry out the task (laboratory, workshop, simulation, debate, oral presentation) and records what they see using a performance rubric, observation scale or checklist. It is essential that the criteria are known in advance so that students know what is expected of their performance.

It is especially useful in experimental sciences, physical education, the arts, vocational training and any area where procedure and attitude are as important as the final result.

**Pedagogical function:** Assess action, decision-making and application.

**When to use:** When the manner of acting matters, not just the result.

**Typical evidence:** Observable performances, simulations, practical work, defences.

**Precautions:** May require complementary evidence.

**Associated media/evidence:** Class participation, Colloquium, Workshop, Simulation, Oral presentation, Project defence, Interview, Structured oral question, Oral test, Practical test, Digital presentation, Video tutorial, Scale model, Prototype, Cooperative work, Socratic seminar, Laboratory checklist, Educational micro-video

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Observation scale, Checklist, Product rubric, Oral rubric, Descriptive record, Participation record, Observation guide

---


### 9. Project-based learning

_Assessment of the process and product of a project._

**Category:** Methodological context · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers/Student

This dimension describes the format or didactic situation in which the evidence is generated. It can combine several collection procedures.

Project-based assessment comprehensively evaluates the process and product of a project: planning, research, creation, communication of results and reflection on what has been learned.

How to apply it: it is advisable to define from the outset what will be assessed in the process (planning, organisation, revisions) and what in the final product (quality, coherence, presentation). Differentiated tools for process and product allow for fairer assessment. Partial submissions and project logbooks help make monitoring continuous and not solely dependent on the final submission.

It allows multiple competencies and criteria to be assessed in an integrated way simultaneously, including teamwork, creativity, research and communication.

**Pedagogical function:** Assess planning, research, creation and communication.

**When to use:** When the task is developed over several days or weeks.

**Typical evidence:** Plan, logbook, partial submissions, final product, defence.

**Precautions:** Requires monitoring to avoid assessing only the final product.

**Associated media/evidence:** Project report, Work plan, Prototype, Project log, Cooperative work

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Individual tracking sheet, Process rubric, Cooperative work rubric, Group tracking sheet, Cooperative work record, Group contract

---


### 10. Inquiry

_Assessment of processes of questioning, searching, hypothesising, data collection and conclusions._

**Category:** Methodological context · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student

This dimension describes the format or didactic situation in which the evidence is generated. It can combine several collection procedures.

Inquiry-based assessment evaluates scientific thinking processes: formulating questions, posing hypotheses, designing procedures, collecting and analysing data, and drawing evidence-based conclusions.

How to apply it: what is assessed is not only the result (whether the hypothesis was correct or incorrect), but the quality of reasoning at each phase of the process. Tools such as inquiry rubrics or process checklists allow each step to be assessed independently. Research reports and logbooks are the most common instruments.

It is especially suitable in natural sciences, social sciences and research projects, where the process of knowledge construction is as important as the result.

**Pedagogical function:** Assess scientific thinking, research and analysis.

**When to use:** In science, social studies and research tasks.

**Typical evidence:** Questions, hypotheses, records, analyses, conclusions.

**Precautions:** May require scaffolding and appropriate sources.

**Associated media/evidence:** Laboratory practice, Laboratory report, Research report, Field notebook

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Correction guide, Checklist

---


### 11. Problem solving

_Assessment of interpretation, planning, solving and justification._

**Category:** Methodological context · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher

This dimension describes the format or didactic situation in which the evidence is generated. It can combine several collection procedures.

Problem-solving assessment evaluates the full process: understanding the problem statement, identifying relevant data, planning the strategy, executing it and verifying the result. It is not enough for the result to be correct; the reasoning and justification also matter.

How to apply it: problems must be contextualised and require genuine application of knowledge, not merely the reproduction of formulas or algorithms. The correction guide or rubric must assess each phase of the process, not just the final answer. Asking students to explain how they solved the problem (in writing or orally) adds valuable information about their reasoning.

Suitable for mathematics, physics, chemistry, technology, economics and any area involving applied problems.

**Pedagogical function:** Assess reasoning and transfer.

**When to use:** In mathematics, science, technology, economics and applied cases.

**Typical evidence:** Procedures, calculations, explanations, decisions.

**Precautions:** The result alone is not enough.

**Associated media/evidence:** Graph or data commentary, Problem solving, Problem-situation, Escape room or gamified challenge

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Correction guide, Descriptive scale

---


### 12. Case study

_Analysis of a real or simulated situation for diagnosis or decision-making._

**Category:** Methodological context · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

This dimension describes the format or didactic situation in which the evidence is generated. It can combine several collection procedures.

Case study presents students with a real or plausible situation that they must analyse, diagnose or make and justify decisions about. It allows for the assessment of applied comprehension, critical judgement and argumentation ability.

How to apply it: the case must be authentic, sufficiently complex and contextualised within the subject area. Students receive the situation and must answer questions or produce a structured analysis. The rubric or analysis guide must assess the quality of reasoning, the selection of relevant information and the coherence of conclusions.

Especially useful in social sciences, health, law, economics, history and any area where real-world application is central.

**Pedagogical function:** Assess applied comprehension, judgement and argumentation.

**When to use:** When applying knowledge to a specific situation is of interest.

**Typical evidence:** Analysis, diagnosis, proposal, justification.

**Precautions:** The quality of the case influences the assessment.

**Associated media/evidence:** Case study

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Analytic rubric, Descriptive scale

### 13. Simulation

_Recreation of a real or plausible situation._

**Category:** Methodological context · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

This dimension describes the format or didactic situation in which the evidence is generated. It can combine several collection procedures.

Simulation-based assessment recreates plausible situations (role play, mock scenarios, role-play performance, interactive practical cases) to observe how students behave in that context. It allows for the assessment of skills that are difficult to observe in ordinary situations.

How to apply it: a scenario is designed with defined roles, information and objectives. The teacher observes using a performance rubric or anecdotal record while the simulation unfolds. It is important to hold a reflective closure where students analyse their own decisions and share them with the group.

Suitable for assessing communication, decision-making, conflict resolution, action protocols and social skills in professional or civic contexts.

**Pedagogical function:** Assess performance, communication and decision-making.

**When to use:** When assessing performance in plausible contexts.

**Typical evidence:** Roles, decisions, responses, interaction.

**Precautions:** Can oversimplify reality.

**Associated media/evidence:** Simulation, Role play, Drama, Escape room or gamified challenge

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Analytic rubric, Observation scale, Anecdotal record

### 14. Digital evidence

_Gathering evidence in digital environments or products._

**Category:** Evidence medium · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

This dimension indicates the medium in which evidence is produced, stored or reviewed.

Assessment through digital evidence gathers and evaluates productions, participation and records generated in digital environments: collaborative documents, forums, digital portfolios, videos, revision histories, platform analytics or contributions to wikis.

How to apply it: it is advisable to define what type of digital evidence will be assessed and with which criteria. The revision history of a shared document, for example, allows individual contributions in group work to be seen. The analytics of an LMS platform can show access times, attempts and study patterns, but should not be used as the sole source of assessment.

Digital evidence allows digital competency to be assessed authentically and provides access to processes that would be invisible on paper. Analytics complement pedagogical judgement, but do not replace it.

**Pedagogical function:** Assess digital competency, production and online participation.

**When to use:** When the activity uses digital tools or leaves online traces.

**Typical evidence:** Documents, forums, videos, websites, activity records.

**Precautions:** Analytics do not replace pedagogical judgement.

**Associated media/evidence:** Digital questionnaire, Infographic, Digital presentation, Video, Podcast, Digital model, Website, Blog post, Discussion forum, Collaborative document, Digital portfolio, Digital notebook, Educational micro-video, AI process log

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Digital rubric, Revision history, Progress dashboard, Assessment spreadsheet, Digital form, Learning analytics, Digital evidence bank, AI and sources use declaration

### 15. Criteria-based assessment

_Assessment of learning in relation to explicit criteria, not by comparison between students._

**Category:** Curricular approach · **Phase:** Design/Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher

Criteria-based assessment evaluates learning against previously defined and communicated criteria. It does not compare students with each other, but analyses to what extent each student demonstrates the expected learning.

How to apply it: the process starts from the assessment criteria, which are specified in observable indicators, appropriate evidence and assessment tools. An evidence map by criterion helps to verify that each assessment decision is supported by sufficient data.

It is especially important in competency-based approaches, because it allows the justification of which evidence demonstrates each criterion and prevents grading from being a sum of disconnected tasks.

**Pedagogical function:** Align evidence, assessment criteria, indicators and grading decisions.

**When to use:** Whenever assessment is to be justified from curricular criteria or defined standards.

**Typical evidence:** Tasks linked to criteria, competency rubrics, evidence maps.

**Precautions:** Requires translating broad criteria into observable indicators without fragmenting learning excessively.

**Associated media/evidence:** Integrated competency task, Problem-situation, E-portfolio with selected evidence, Open-book test

**Recommended assessment instruments:** List of indicators per criterion, Evidence map by criterion, Competency rubric, Criteria grading table, AI and sources use declaration

---


### 16. Inclusive assessment and UDL

_Design of assessment that is accessible, flexible and adjusted to student diversity._

**Category:** Curricular approach · **Phase:** Design/Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student

Inclusive assessment, aligned with Universal Design for Learning, ensures that all students can demonstrate what they know and are able to do through accessible and varied evidence.

How to apply it: barriers of comprehension, expression, time, format or context are anticipated; reasonable supports and alternatives are offered; and the criteria being assessed remain clear. For example, the same criterion can be evidenced through text, oral presentation, visual product or practical demonstration if all of them allow the same learning to be observed.

Its purpose is to increase the validity of assessment: so that the result reflects learning and not the barriers of the chosen format.

**Pedagogical function:** Reduce barriers and offer different ways of demonstrating learning.

**When to use:** In any diverse group, especially when a single form of evidence may conceal real learning.

**Typical evidence:** Alternative products, supports, scaffolding, access adaptations, guided self-assessments.

**Precautions:** Flexibility must not lower the criteria, but rather diversify pathways and supports for evidencing them.

**Associated media/evidence:** Integrated competency task, Digital notebook, E-portfolio with selected evidence, Oral presentation, Video, Podcast, Infographic, Concept map, Practical test, Laboratory practice, Interview, Learning portfolio, Collaborative document, Reflection sheet, Exit ticket, Learning traffic light

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Progress scale, Feedback/feedforward template, Evidence map by criterion

---


### 17. Continuous assessment

_Gathering and interpreting evidence throughout the learning process._

**Category:** Purpose and timing · **Phase:** Initial/Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student

Continuous assessment interprets evidence gathered at different moments to understand student progress. It does not consist of grading everything, but of using successive evidence to make better decisions.

How to apply it: information-gathering milestones are planned, relevant progress is recorded and feedback is returned while there is still room for improvement. Partial submissions, the digital notebook, the portfolio and progress scales are especially useful.

Well used, it allows assessment to be part of learning and not just a check at the end.

**Pedagogical function:** Track progress, detect needs and adjust teaching in a sustained way.

**When to use:** In long units, projects, competency-based learning and ordinary course monitoring.

**Typical evidence:** Drafts, partial submissions, journals, digital notebook, progress records.

**Precautions:** Must not be confused with accumulating many grades; it requires selecting significant evidence.

**Associated media/evidence:** Digital notebook, E-portfolio with selected evidence, Partial submission, Draft

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Progress scale, Progress dashboard, Digital evidence bank, Individual tracking sheet

---


### 18. Feedback and feedforward

_Information to understand current performance and guide the next improvement._

**Category:** Purpose and use · **Phase:** Process · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

Feedback explains to students where they stand in relation to the objective, what they have done well, what they need to improve and what the next step is. Feedforward adds guidance towards future tasks, not just correction of what has already been submitted.

How to apply it: brief comments, cards, individual conferences, draft reviews or guided peer assessment are used. The essential thing is that students can act on the information received.

It has more impact when it arrives during the process, focuses on specific criteria and avoids generic messages such as 'well done' or 'you need to try harder'.

**Pedagogical function:** Turn assessment into concrete improvement action.

**When to use:** When students can revise, repeat, improve or transfer what they have learned to a subsequent task.

**Typical evidence:** Comments, feedback cards, improvement commitments, draft reviews.

**Precautions:** Must be specific, timely and actionable; a grade alone is not sufficient feedback.

**Associated media/evidence:** Draft, Peer review, Two stars and a wish, Partial submission, E-portfolio with selected evidence, AI process log

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Feedback/feedforward template, Feedback card, Improvement commitments record, AI and sources use declaration

---


### 19. Grading and accreditation

_Justified transformation of evidence into a grade, promotion or certification decision._

**Category:** Purpose and use · **Phase:** Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Teaching team

Grading and accreditation convert learning evidence into a communicable decision: a grade, a level of achievement, a promotion or a certification.

How to apply it: it is made explicit which criteria count, what evidence supports them and how they are combined. Criteria grading tables, weighting matrices, records and individual reports help to make the decision traceable.

It is a necessary function of the educational system, but must be distinguished from formative feedback: grading informs about the level achieved; guiding improvement requires additional information.

**Pedagogical function:** Communicate the level achieved and support academic decisions with evidence.

**When to use:** When closing units, assessments, courses or processes requiring an administrative decision.

**Typical evidence:** Tests, final tasks, rubrics, criteria tables, records and reports.

**Precautions:** Must be supported by sufficient criteria and evidence; does not replace formative assessment.

**Associated media/evidence:** Contextualised competency test, Integrated competency task, E-portfolio with selected evidence

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Criteria grading table, Weighting matrix, Individual assessment report, Assessment record, Evidence map by criterion

---


### 20. Shared and participatory assessment

_Student participation in criteria, evidence, review and improvement decisions._

**Category:** Assessment agent · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

Shared and participatory assessment involves students in different parts of the assessment process: understanding or constructing criteria, reviewing evidence, carrying out peer assessment, self-assessing and proposing improvements.

How to apply it: indicators can be agreed upon, group contracts used, drafts reviewed by peers, or self-assessment compared with the teacher's assessment. The final responsibility for grading remains with the teacher when the system requires it.

It is especially valuable because it makes the criteria visible and turns assessment into learning about the quality of one's own work.

**Pedagogical function:** Increase understanding of criteria, responsibility and self-regulation.

**When to use:** In projects, cooperative work, portfolios and tasks where students can review their process.

**Typical evidence:** Criteria negotiation, peer assessment, group contracts, improvement commitments.

**Precautions:** Requires clear criteria and support; teacher responsibility must not be delegated without guidance.

**Associated media/evidence:** Cooperative work, Peer review, Learning contract, E-portfolio with selected evidence, Escape room or gamified challenge, Socratic seminar

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Group contract, Peer assessment card, Peer assessment rubric, Improvement commitments record, Sociogram

---


### 21. Gamified assessment

_Use of game mechanics to collect evidence of learning._

**Category:** Methodological context · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

This dimension describes the didactic format or situation in which evidence is generated. It can combine several collection procedures.

Gamified assessment incorporates game mechanics (challenges, points, badges, countdowns, regulated cooperation or competition) to collect evidence of learning in a highly motivating environment. It is not synonymous with a specific instrument such as an escape room (INS_ESCAPE), but rather the methodological approach that encompasses any strategy using games for assessment purposes.

How to apply it: tasks or challenges are designed with clear rules, visible achievement criteria and immediate feedback. Points or badges can be used as evidence of the level reached if they are aligned with real assessment criteria. Analytics from the gamified platform (time, attempts, correct answers) complement the teacher's observation but do not replace it.

It is especially suitable for formative assessment, consolidation and diagnostic assessment, as the playful context reduces assessment anxiety and encourages participation from students who struggle with motivation.

**Pedagogical function:** Increase motivation and engagement, and obtain immediate feedback on learning.

**When to use:** When high motivation is sought, when consolidating learning, or when conducting active diagnostic assessment.

**Typical evidence:** Challenges, escape rooms, timed rounds, badges, leaderboards, cooperative quests.

**Precautions:** Game mechanics must not distort assessment; participation must be distinguished from demonstrated learning.

**Associated media/evidence:** Escape room or gamified challenge, Problem solving, Problem-situation, Integrated competency task, Digital questionnaire

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Observation scale, Checklist, Competency rubric, Learning analytics

---


### 22. Traceability, authorship and AI use

_Documentation of the process, sources, external support and AI use behind a piece of evidence._

**Category:** Evidence medium · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Participation:** Teacher/Student

This dimension places assessment in contexts where the production process may be hidden or supported by external tools, including generative AI.

How to apply it: students document which sources, supports, tools or AI systems they used, what they used them for and which decisions were their own. They may keep versions, relevant prompts, changes made, source checks and brief justifications. The teacher assesses the quality of the process, critical review and the match between the final product and the learning demonstrated.

It is especially useful in research tasks, projects, collaborative documents, portfolios, digital products and tasks completed partly outside the classroom. Its purpose is not to penalise AI use, but to make it transparent and assessable.

**Pedagogical function:** Make the production process visible and distinguish demonstrated learning from the support used.

**When to use:** When a task allows external help, digital editing, work outside the classroom or generative AI use.

**Typical evidence:** Versions, prompts, sources, revision history, justified decisions, AI-use declaration.

**Precautions:** It should not become bureaucracy; traceability must provide real evidence about decisions, review and authorship.

**Associated media/evidence:** Learning diary, Learning portfolio, Research report, Monograph, Essay, Popular science article, Project report, Work plan, Collaborative document, Digital portfolio, Project log, Digital notebook, E-portfolio with selected evidence, Digital presentation, Website, Blog post, Video, Video tutorial, Educational micro-video, Text commentary, Graph or data commentary, Review, Problem solving, Case study, Contextualised competency test, Infographic, Academic poster, Podcast, Digital model, Discussion forum, Draft, Partial submission, Peer review, Field notebook, Summary, Outline, Concept map, Mind map, Timeline, Glossary, Reading card, Meeting minutes, Reflection sheet, Self-report, Integrated competency task, Problem-situation, AI process log

**Recommended assessment instruments:** AI and sources use declaration, Revision history, Evidence map by criterion, Digital evidence bank, Process rubric, Feedback card, Learning analytics

## Assessable Evidence

### 1. Individual classroom work

_Observation of the student working individually on tasks in class._

**Type:** Observation · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Low/Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

Individual classroom work refers to any task the student performs alone during class time: exercises, readings, responses, problems, etc. The teacher can observe the process (how the student approaches the task) and the product (the completed work).

How to use it for assessment: combined with an observation guide or anecdotal record, it allows collecting information about pace, autonomy, persistence and problem-solving strategies.

It is especially useful for formative assessment and for detecting learning difficulties early.

**Evidence:** Observable performance

**Suitable for:** Autonomy; effort; pace; individual progress.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation, Incidental observation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Grade book, Observation scale, Individual tracking sheet, Checklist

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose

### 2. Cooperative work

_Observation of students working in a structured group with defined roles._

**Type:** Observation · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium/High · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

Cooperative work involves structured activities in which students work in teams with defined roles, shared goals and individual accountability. The teacher can observe interaction, role-play and contribution to the group product.

How to use it for assessment: a cooperative work rubric allows evaluating both the process (participation, communication, conflict resolution) and the product. Peer assessment adds a complementary perspective.

It is important to evaluate individual contributions and not only the group result.

**Evidence:** Group performance

**Suitable for:** Collaboration; communication; shared responsibility.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation, Incidental observation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Teacher's diary, Peer assessment card, Cooperative work rubric, Group contract, Sociogram, Observation guide

**Associated dimensions:** Peer assessment, Formative purpose, Performance evidence, Project-based learning, Teacher assessment, Shared and participatory assessment

### 3. Class participation

_Observation of the quality and frequency of student contributions in class._

**Type:** Observation · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Low/Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

Class participation refers to the student's contributions during group sessions: questions, responses, interventions, debates and collaborative interactions. It is assessed observationally by the teacher.

How to use it for assessment: a participation record or observation scale allows noting frequency and quality of contributions. It is important to define criteria clearly (relevance, argumentative quality, respect for turns) and avoid confusing participation with extroversion.

It is best used as a formative indicator rather than a standalone summative mark.

**Evidence:** Observable performance

**Suitable for:** Engagement; oral communication; critical thinking.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation, Incidental observation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Grade book, Teacher's diary, Observation scale, Participation record

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose, Performance evidence

### 4. Debate

_Structured oral confrontation of opposing positions with evidence and arguments._

**Type:** Oral · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

A debate is a structured oral activity in which two or more students or groups defend opposing positions on a given topic. It requires preparation, use of evidence and respect for speaking turns.

How to use it for assessment: an oral rubric or observation guide allows evaluating the quality of arguments, use of evidence, clarity, respect for turns and responsiveness to opposing arguments.

It is important to assess both the preparation process and the oral performance, and to separate the quality of the argument from agreement with the teacher's position.

**Evidence:** Oral performance

**Suitable for:** Argumentation; critical thinking; respectful communication.

**Associated techniques:** Oral exchanges, Systematic observation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Observation scale, Peer assessment card

**Associated dimensions:** Peer assessment

### 5. Colloquium

_Open group dialogue to explore and share understanding of a topic._

**Type:** Oral · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

A colloquium is an open group dialogue — less formal than a debate — in which students share interpretations, doubts and reflections on a topic. The teacher facilitates the conversation and can guide it with probing questions.

How to use it for assessment: an observation guide or participation record allows noting the quality of contributions, conceptual depth and listening skills.

It is less suitable for individual summative assessment but very valuable as a formative tool to detect misunderstandings.

**Evidence:** Oral performance

**Suitable for:** Collective understanding; conceptual depth; shared construction.

**Associated techniques:** Oral exchanges

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Observation scale, Anecdotal record

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose, Performance evidence

### 6. Assembly

_Participatory group space for sharing, deciding and reflecting collectively._

**Type:** Oral · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

An assembly is a participatory group space in which students share opinions, make decisions and reflect collectively on life in the classroom or learning processes. It is common in primary education but also applicable in secondary.

How to use it for assessment: through a participation record or observation guide, the teacher can note communicative competencies, democratic values and quality of reflections.

It has significant formative value but is difficult to use for individual summative assessment.

**Evidence:** Oral performance

**Suitable for:** Democratic participation; decision-making; collective reflection.

**Associated techniques:** Oral exchanges, Systematic observation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Participation record, Observation scale

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose, Peer assessment

### 7. Laboratory practice

_Practical activity in which students apply scientific procedures in a laboratory context._

**Type:** Practical activity · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher

Laboratory practice is a hands-on activity in which students apply experimental or scientific procedures: taking measurements, handling materials, recording data and drawing conclusions. The teacher can observe the process and assess the laboratory report.

How to use it for assessment: an observation guide allows evaluating safety, procedure and technique; a process rubric assesses the quality of the report and conclusions.

It is important to assess both the practical process and the written output.

**Evidence:** Practical performance

**Suitable for:** Scientific procedure; data handling; scientific thinking.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Teacher's diary, Observation scale, Checklist

**Associated dimensions:** Inquiry, Teacher assessment

### 8. Workshop

_Practical and collaborative activity focused on creating a product or developing a skill._

**Type:** Practical activity · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium/High · **Participation:** Teacher

A workshop is a practical and collaborative learning space focused on creating a product (a craft, a text, a programme) or developing a specific skill. The teacher can observe the process and assess the resulting product.

How to use it for assessment: a process rubric evaluates the work process; a product rubric evaluates the finished result. A self-assessment sheet helps students reflect on their learning.

It is particularly suitable for competency-based assessment because it integrates knowledge, skills and attitudes.

**Evidence:** Practical performance

**Suitable for:** Applied skills; creative process; hands-on competencies.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Checklist, Observation scale, Analytic rubric

**Associated dimensions:** Performance evidence

### 9. Simulation

_Recreation of a real or hypothetical situation to apply knowledge and skills._

**Type:** Performance · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

A simulation recreates a real or hypothetical situation in which students must apply knowledge and skills to solve a problem or carry out a task: medical simulations, business negotiations, historical reconstructions, etc.

How to use it for assessment: an analytic rubric allows evaluating different dimensions of performance (decision-making, communication, use of knowledge). An observation guide records key moments.

It is demanding to design and assess, but offers high validity for competency-based assessment.

**Evidence:** Contextualised performance

**Suitable for:** Decision-making; application in context; professional skills.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Observation scale, Analytic rubric, Descriptive record

### 10. Role play

_Activity in which students take on specific roles to explore situations or demonstrate competencies._

**Type:** Performance · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium/High · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

Role play involves students taking on specific roles — historical figures, professionals, fictional characters — to explore situations, practise skills or demonstrate competencies in a simulated context.

How to use it for assessment: an oral rubric or observation guide allows evaluating quality of interpretation, use of relevant content and communication.

It is important to clearly define what is being assessed (the content, the performance or both) and to avoid penalising shyness.

**Evidence:** Oral/group performance

**Suitable for:** Empathy; oral communication; situational application.

**Associated techniques:** Oral exchanges

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Analytic rubric, Anecdotal record, Observation scale

### 11. Drama

_Theatrical or expressive performance to communicate, interpret or explore content._

**Type:** Performance · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

Drama involves students creating or staging a theatrical piece to communicate, interpret or explore content. Unlike role play, it typically involves greater preparation, script work and group coordination.

How to use it for assessment: an oral rubric or performance observation guide allows evaluating expressive quality, understanding of the content and teamwork.

It is important to separate the quality of the performance from artistic talent and define the criteria clearly beforehand.

**Evidence:** Oral/physical performance

**Suitable for:** Oral and bodily expression; creativity; collective work.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Observation scale, Oral rubric

### 12. Oral presentation

_Structured oral exposition of content, project or work before an audience._

**Type:** Oral · **Phase:** Final · **Complexity:** Medium/High · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

An oral presentation is a structured activity in which a student or group presents content, a project or a work to an audience. It involves planning, organisation and oral communication.

How to use it for assessment: an oral rubric allows evaluating clarity of exposition, structure, use of resources and command of the topic. Peer assessment can add a complementary perspective.

It is important to assess the content and not only the presentation style, and to adapt criteria to the educational level.

**Evidence:** Oral performance

**Suitable for:** Oral communication; organisation of ideas; subject mastery.

**Associated techniques:** Oral exchanges, Systematic observation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Descriptor bank, Level-to-grade converter, Observation scale, Checklist, Oral rubric

### 13. Project defence

_Oral presentation and defence of a project before a panel or audience._

**Type:** Oral · **Phase:** Final · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

A project defence involves students presenting their project and responding to questions from the teacher or a panel. It combines oral presentation with the ability to justify decisions, respond to objections and demonstrate deep understanding.

How to use it for assessment: an oral rubric assesses the presentation; an evaluative interview guide assesses responses to questions. It can be individual or group.

It is one of the most complete forms of assessment but requires clear preparation of criteria and adequate time.

**Evidence:** Oral performance

**Suitable for:** Command of the project; argumentation; response to questions.

**Associated techniques:** Oral exchanges, Analysis of productions, Evaluative interview

### 14. Interview

_Structured or semi-structured dialogue between teacher and student to assess learning._

**Type:** Oral · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

An evaluative interview is a structured or semi-structured conversation between teacher and student to explore understanding, reasoning and learning processes. Unlike questioning in class, it is individual and planned.

How to use it for assessment: an interview guide with open questions allows evaluating depth of understanding. Responses can be recorded in an anecdotal record.

It is particularly useful for detecting difficulties, assessing metacognition and providing personalised feedback, but is time-demanding.

**Evidence:** Oral response

**Suitable for:** Deep understanding; reasoning processes; individual feedback.

**Associated techniques:** Oral exchanges, Evaluative interview

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Anecdotal record, Observation scale

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose, Performance evidence

### 15. Structured oral question

_Targeted oral question to check understanding or reasoning during class._

**Type:** Oral · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Low/Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

A structured oral question is a targeted question directed at a student or the group to verify understanding, reasoning or application of content. It differs from open questions in that it is deliberate and focused on specific learning criteria.

How to use it for assessment: an anecdotal record or brief observation note allows registering responses and patterns. It is one of the most immediate and frequent formative assessment tools.

It is important to avoid assessing only those who raise their hand and to distribute questions equitably among students.

**Evidence:** Oral response

**Suitable for:** Immediate checking; detecting misunderstandings; active engagement.

**Associated techniques:** Oral exchanges, Probing and questioning

### 16. Class notebook

**Type:** Written production · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The class notebook is the student's personal record of activities, notes and work done in class over time. It reflects organisation, neatness, continuity and understanding of content.

How to use it for assessment: a checklist or descriptive scale allows evaluating completeness, organisation and quality of content. It can be complemented with corrections and personalised feedback.

It is a simple and accessible tool, though it tends to reflect effort and organisation more than deep learning.

**Evidence:** Written production

**Suitable for:** Organisation; continuity; record of learning process.

### 17. Learning diary

**Type:** Written reflection · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Student

The learning diary is a personal written record in which the student regularly notes what they have learned, how they have learned it, difficulties encountered and proposed improvements. It prioritises the reflective process over the product.

How to use it for assessment: a reflection sheet or assessment scale allows evaluating depth of reflection, self-regulation capacity and metacognitive awareness. The teacher can provide written feedback on selected entries.

It is a powerful tool for formative assessment but requires teaching students what a genuine reflection is.

**Evidence:** Written reflection

**Suitable for:** Metacognition; self-regulation; awareness of learning process.

It is motivating for students who learn better through visual and oral formats than through writing. It develops digital competence, communication skills and the ability to synthesise.

**Evidence:** Audiovisual product

**Suitable for:** Synthesis; concise communication; creativity; digital competence.

**Associated techniques:** Analysis of productions, Oral exchanges

**Recommended assessment instruments:** Analytic rubric, Product rubric, Checklist, Peer assessment card, AI and sources use declaration

**Associated dimensions:** Digital evidence, Competency-based approach, Performance evidence, Traceability, authorship and AI use

### 86. AI process log

_Record of the work process, AI use, sources, versions and decisions made._

**Type:** Digital/process · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher/Student

The AI process log is evidence in which the student documents how they produced a task when digital tools, external sources or generative AI could be used. It records the work journey, not only the final product.

How to use it for assessment: students record relevant steps, sources consulted, prompts or instructions used, responses used or discarded, changes between versions, checks carried out and their own decisions. They do not need to keep everything, only enough evidence to explain the process.

It supports assessment of authorship, critical thinking, revision, improvement and responsible AI use. It should be linked to specific criteria so that it does not become a formal declaration with no assessment value.

**Evidence:** Process record

**Suitable for:** Projects; research tasks; digital productions; AI-supported tasks; work outside the classroom.

**Associated techniques:** Analysis of processes and reflections, Documentary analysis, Analysis of digital interactions, Evidence triangulation

**Recommended assessment instruments:** AI and sources use declaration, Revision history, Evidence map by criterion, Process rubric, Feedback card, Digital evidence bank

## Assessment Instruments

### 1. Analytic rubric

_Matrix that assesses separate criteria using described levels._

**Type:** Qualitative assessment/grading · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

The analytic rubric is a matrix that breaks the task down into separate criteria and describes the performance levels for each. Each criterion is assessed independently, allowing precise feedback on which aspects are strong and which need improvement.

How to use it: design a table where rows are the criteria (e.g., 'structure', 'argumentation', 'linguistic accuracy') and columns are the levels (e.g., 'excellent', 'satisfactory', 'developing', 'beginning'). Each cell contains a descriptor explaining what performance at that level looks like for that criterion. When assessing, the teacher reads each row and marks the level that best describes what was observed.

It is the most suitable assessment tool for complex tasks where detailed feedback matters. It requires design time, but once created it can be reused and shared with students as a guide.

**Useful for:** Assessing complex tasks with multiple aspects.

**Suitable for:** Projects, reports, presentations, debates, digital products.

**Advantages:** Clarifies expectations and allows precise feedback.

**Limitations:** Requires design time and good descriptors.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation, Analysis of productions, Specific tests

**Compatible media/evidence:** Laboratory report, Project defence, Oral presentation, Debate, Video, Laboratory practice, Workshop, Simulation, Role play, Monograph, Text commentary, Graph or data commentary, Concept map, Review, Popular science article, Case study, Written development test, Document-based test, Infographic, Digital presentation, Video tutorial, Comic, Scale model, Digital model, Blog post, Collaborative document, Open-book test, Problem-situation, Educational micro-video

**Associated dimensions:** Teacher assessment, Summative purpose, Case study, Simulation

---


### 2. Global or holistic rubric

_Rubric that assesses performance holistically._

**Type:** Qualitative assessment/grading · **Phase:** Final · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The holistic rubric assesses performance globally, offering a description of each level that covers the task as a whole without separating individual criteria. The assessor selects the level that best describes the work in its entirety.

How to use it: write 3–5 global descriptions ordered from highest to lowest quality. When assessing, read the complete work and select the description that best fits. It is faster than the analytic rubric but offers less diagnostic information.

Suitable when time is limited, when the task is short, or when a quick overall assessment is needed. For work where detailed feedback is a priority, the analytic rubric is more appropriate.

**Useful for:** Assessing a complete task without separating all criteria.

**Suitable for:** Overall products, brief performances, final assessments.

**Advantages:** Faster than an analytic rubric.

**Limitations:** Offers less diagnostic information.

**Compatible media/evidence:** Essay, Oral presentation, Project defence

**Associated dimensions:** Summative purpose

---


### 3. Process rubric

_Rubric focused on planning, monitoring, revision and improvement._

**Type:** Formative assessment · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher/Student

The process rubric focuses assessment on how the task is developed: planning, organisation, revisions, time management, decision-making and use of resources. It does not assess the final product, but the path taken to reach it.

How to use it: criteria include aspects such as 'defines clear objectives at the outset', 'records progress and difficulties', 'reviews and adjusts the plan when necessary' or 'manages the group's time well'. It is applied during the development of the project, not at the end.

It is essential in long-duration projects, research and learning portfolios, where the learning process is as valuable as the result. It enables difficulties to be identified early and feedback to be given while there is still time to act.

**Useful for:** Assessing how a task is developed.

**Suitable for:** Projects, research, learning portfolios, project logs.

**Advantages:** Makes the process visible, not just the result.

**Limitations:** Requires ongoing monitoring.

**Associated techniques:** Analysis of processes and reflections

**Compatible media/evidence:** Project defence, Digital portfolio, Project log, Partial submission, AI process log

**Associated dimensions:** Project-based learning, Traceability, authorship and AI use

---


### 4. Product rubric

_Rubric focused on the quality of the final product._

**Type:** Assessment/grading · **Phase:** Final · **Complexity:** Medium/High · **Participation:** Teacher

The product rubric evaluates the characteristics of the final artefact submitted: quality, structure, content, presentation, coherence and compliance with requirements. Unlike the process rubric, it does not consider how the result was achieved.

How to use it: criteria focus on the product itself: 'the information is accurate and relevant', 'the structure is clear and coherent', 'the format is appropriate for the audience'. Each criterion has described levels that allow the work to be located.

It complements the process rubric in extended projects. Used alone, it may lead to assessing only the result and not the learning.

**Useful for:** Assessing the final result.

**Suitable for:** Written, digital, visual or physical products.

**Advantages:** Allows quality to be assessed with clear criteria.

**Limitations:** May leave the process out if used alone.

**Compatible media/evidence:** Infographic, Laboratory report, Scale model, Video, Website, Prototype, Educational micro-video

**Associated dimensions:** Summative purpose, Performance evidence

---


### 5. Oral rubric

_Rubric for presentations, debates, defences or interviews._

**Type:** Performance assessment · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** Medium/High · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

The oral rubric is an analytic rubric designed specifically to assess oral performances: presentations, debates, project defences, talks, interviews or colloquia.

How to use it: common criteria include 'clarity and organisation of the discourse', 'use of subject-specific vocabulary', 'eye contact and posture', 'response to questions' and 'adherence to time limits'. During the performance, the assessor marks the level of each criterion while observing. If there are many students, the performance can be recorded for later review (with the necessary permissions).

Sharing it before the performance allows students to prepare more effectively and reduces anxiety by knowing exactly what will be assessed.

**Useful for:** Assessing oral communication, content and interaction.

**Suitable for:** Oral performances.

**Advantages:** Facilitates observation of diverse aspects of oral communication.

**Limitations:** Can be difficult to record everything live.

**Associated techniques:** Oral exchanges, Evaluative interview

**Compatible media/evidence:** Debate, Project defence, Interview, Oral presentation, Podcast, Drama, Oral test, Discussion forum, Socratic seminar

**Associated dimensions:** Performance evidence, Peer assessment, Summative purpose

---


### 6. Cooperative work rubric

_Rubric for assessing collaboration and group functioning._

**Type:** Process assessment · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

The cooperative work rubric assesses the quality of collaboration within the group: equitable participation, communication, individual responsibility, conflict resolution and contribution to the shared goal.

How to use it: it can be applied by the teacher through observation, by the student themselves through self-assessment, or by peers through peer assessment (or all three at once). Criteria should distinguish between observable behaviours ('participates actively in discussions', 'completes assigned tasks') and group outcomes.

It is especially useful for preventing group work from diluting individual responsibility and for giving feedback on social and collaborative skills.

**Useful for:** Assessing contribution, roles, responsibility and communication.

**Suitable for:** Group work and projects.

**Advantages:** Helps assess the group process.

**Limitations:** Requires evidence of real participation.

**Compatible media/evidence:** Cooperative work, Project defence, Collaborative document, Escape room or gamified challenge

**Associated dimensions:** Peer assessment, Project-based learning

---


### 7. Competency rubric

_Matrix linked to criteria and competency performance levels._

**Type:** Competency assessment · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher

The competency rubric is designed to assess the degree of development of a specific competency (communicative, mathematical, digital, scientific, etc.) through a contextualised task. Its criteria are aligned with the operational descriptors of the educational level.

How to use it: criteria reflect the key elements of the competency (e.g., for the communicative competency: 'understands complex texts', 'produces texts appropriate to the context', 'argues with evidence'). Each criterion has 3–4 described levels. It can be used to assess a single task or as a cumulative profile over the course.

It is the most suitable assessment tool for competency-based assessment required by the current curricular framework.

**Useful for:** Assessing the mobilisation of knowledge in context.

**Suitable for:** Contextualised competency tasks.

**Advantages:** Aligns assessment with criteria and competencies.

**Limitations:** Must formulate observable indicators.

**Compatible media/evidence:** Project defence, Case study, Problem solving, Simulation, Contextualised competency test, Integrated competency task

**Associated dimensions:** Competency-based approach, Criteria-based assessment

---


### 8. Checklist

_List of indicators with presence or absence._

**Type:** Record/assessment · **Phase:** Initial/Process/Final · **Complexity:** Low · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

The checklist is an assessment tool that lists the elements, steps or behaviours that must be present in a task or performance. For each item, the assessor simply marks whether it is present (yes/no) or has been fulfilled.

How to use it: draw up a list of concrete, observable indicators (e.g., 'includes an introduction', 'cites sources', 'uses safety equipment'). During or after the activity, mark each item. There are no intermediate levels; it is a binary check.

Fast to apply and very useful for activities with clear, closed requirements: laboratory protocols, presentations with defined requirements, technical procedures. For tasks with qualitative nuances, a rubric or scale is preferable.

**Useful for:** Checking requirements, steps or specific evidence.

**Suitable for:** Simple tasks or clear procedures.

**Advantages:** Fast, clear and easy to use.

**Limitations:** Does not assess quality or nuance well.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation, Oral exchanges, Probing and questioning

**Compatible media/evidence:** Exit ticket, Class notebook, Laboratory report, Laboratory practice, Learning traffic light, Individual classroom work, Workshop, Oral presentation, Summary, Outline, Concept map, Mind map, Timeline, Glossary, Reading card, Work plan, Meeting minutes, Practical test, Infographic, Digital presentation, Video tutorial, Scale model, Website, Initial concept map, Draft, Laboratory checklist

**Associated dimensions:** Self-assessment, Teacher assessment, Formative purpose, Performance evidence, Inquiry

---


### 9. Observation scale

_Scale for assessing frequency, intensity or degree of a performance._

**Type:** Record/assessment · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The observation scale records the degree to which a behaviour, attitude or skill is displayed during an activity. Unlike the checklist (yes/no), it offers several points on the scale (e.g., always / almost always / sometimes / rarely / never).

How to use it: define the indicators to be observed and choose a frequency or quality scale. During the activity, the teacher marks the observed level for each student. It can be designed to observe several students at once if the indicators are few and specific.

Useful for assessing participation, attitudes, procedures and social skills in group activities, debates, practical activities and workshops.

**Useful for:** Recording observable performances.

**Suitable for:** Participation, cooperative work, practical activities, presentations.

**Advantages:** Allows performance to be graded.

**Limitations:** Can be ambiguous without clear descriptors.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation, Oral exchanges

**Compatible media/evidence:** Debate, Project defence, Oral presentation, Laboratory practice, Structured oral question, Cooperative work, Individual classroom work, Class participation, Colloquium, Assembly, Workshop, Simulation, Role play, Drama, Interview, Class notebook, Graph or data commentary, Outline, Mind map, Reading card, Reflection sheet, Self-report, Academic poster, Comic, Digital model, Blog post, Project log

**Associated dimensions:** Peer assessment, Teacher assessment, Formative purpose, Performance evidence, Simulation

### 10. Rating scale

_Graduated scale for assessing degree of achievement._

**Type:** Assessment · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher/Student/Peers

The rating scale assigns a numerical or qualitative value to each criterion assessed, without reaching the precision of a rubric in describing what each level means. It is an intermediate point between the checklist and the analytic rubric.

How to use it: list the criteria and place a scale next to each one (e.g., 1–4 or insufficient/sufficient/good/excellent). The assessor assigns a value to each criterion based on their judgement. It is faster than a rubric because it does not require detailed descriptors for each cell.

Suitable when the teacher has enough experience to apply criteria implicitly, but may be less reliable and less informative for students than a rubric with descriptors.

**Useful for:** Assessing achievements with levels.

**Suitable for:** Tasks of low or medium complexity.

**Advantages:** Flexible and easy to apply.

**Limitations:** Less precise than a full rubric.

**Compatible media/evidence:** Class notebook, Class participation, Podcast, Summary, Video

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose, Summative purpose

---


### 11. Descriptive scale

_Scale whose levels include descriptions._

**Type:** Qualitative assessment · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The descriptive scale is a variant of the rating scale in which each point on the scale is accompanied by a verbal description explaining what characterises that level. It is more precise than the numerical scale but less detailed than the analytic rubric.

How to use it: for each criterion, write 3–4 descriptions ordered from highest to lowest quality. When assessing, read the description that best corresponds to the observed work. It can be combined with a numerical score associated with each level.

It is a good starting point for teachers who want to introduce more explicit criteria without investing the time required to design a full analytic rubric.

**Useful for:** Assessing quality with greater clarity than a numerical scale.

**Suitable for:** Open tasks of medium complexity.

**Advantages:** Provides qualitative nuance.

**Limitations:** Requires writing descriptors.

**Associated techniques:** Analysis of productions

**Compatible media/evidence:** Text commentary, Oral presentation, Problem solving, Learning portfolio, Summary, Popular science article

**Associated dimensions:** Problem solving, Case study

---


### 12. Correction guide

_Document with criteria, expected answers or marking guidelines._

**Type:** Correction/grading · **Phase:** Final · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The correction guide specifies what should be found in a correct or quality response: which concepts, which arguments, which steps, which elements. It guides the marking of written, oral or practical tests.

How to use it: before marking, the teacher draws up a guide listing the expected points in each question or task, indicating how many marks each is worth. When marking, the student's response is compared with the guide and the corresponding score is assigned. It can include examples of acceptable responses or common errors.

It increases the consistency of marking across different marking sessions by the same teacher or across different teachers marking the same exam.

**Useful for:** Marking tests or tasks with common criteria.

**Suitable for:** Tests, commentaries, problems, reports.

**Advantages:** Standardises marking.

**Limitations:** Less suitable for very open tasks if not adapted.

**Associated techniques:** Analysis of productions, Specific tests, Evaluative interview

**Compatible media/evidence:** Written development test, Problem solving, Text commentary, Laboratory report, Research report, Monograph, Essay, Glossary, Review, Case study, Short-answer test, Contextualised competency test, Document-based test, Open-book test, Problem-situation, Field notebook

**Associated dimensions:** Teacher assessment, Summative purpose, Inquiry, Problem solving

---


### 13. Correction template

_Format for recording scores or correct answers._

**Type:** Correction/grading · **Phase:** Initial/Final · **Complexity:** Low · **Participation:** Teacher

The correction template is a structured document that organises the marking task: it lists the questions or tasks, indicates the maximum score for each, and provides space to record the score obtained and observations.

How to use it: it is prepared before marking and used as a recording sheet during the process. It can include model answers, general criteria and notes on typical errors detected during marking.

Especially useful when there are many tests to mark or when several teachers are marking the same instrument, as it guarantees uniformity and speeds up the process.

**Useful for:** Marking quickly and consistently.

**Suitable for:** Objective tests or short-answer tests.

**Advantages:** Facilitates calculation and review.

**Limitations:** Can reduce assessment to correct answers.

**Associated techniques:** Specific tests, Survey and questionnaire

**Compatible media/evidence:** Multiple-choice test, Digital questionnaire, Short-answer test, Objective test

**Associated dimensions:** Summative purpose

---


### 14. Scoring scale

_Distribution of points among sections or indicators._

**Type:** Grading · **Phase:** Final · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The scoring scale is a scoring system that establishes how much each part of a test, task or set of criteria is worth, and how partial scores are translated into an overall mark.

How to use it: define the maximum score for each question, exercise or criterion, and indicate the formula for calculating the final mark (direct sum, weighting, rounding). If there is a penalty for errors, it must be clearly specified. The scoring scale should be made public before the test so that students know how much each part is worth.

A well-designed scoring scale reflects the relative importance of each content area or competency assessed and facilitates objective grading.

**Useful for:** Assigning scores.

**Suitable for:** Tests and tasks with defined scoring.

**Advantages:** Makes the distribution of marks explicit.

**Limitations:** Can become mechanical without qualitative criteria.

**Associated techniques:** Specific tests

**Compatible media/evidence:** Written development test, Problem solving, Laboratory report, Text commentary, Objective test, Multiple-choice test, Short-answer test, Open-book test

**Associated dimensions:** Summative purpose

### 15. Anecdotal record

_Brief note of significant observed events._

**Type:** Qualitative record · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Low/Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The anecdotal record is a document where the teacher briefly and with dates notes significant events observed in students: notable progress, a persistent difficulty, outstanding behaviour, a relevant contribution.

How to use it: it can be a notebook, a card per student, an app or a sheet divided by student. The key is to record the event as observed (without interpreting it yet) and add the date. Over time, the accumulation of anecdotes reveals patterns of learning or behaviour that would otherwise go unnoticed.

Especially useful in stages without numerical grading, for completing descriptive reports, guiding tutorials and grounding continuous formative assessment.

**Useful for:** Collecting relevant evidence not always anticipated.

**Suitable for:** Behaviours, progress, difficulties, incidents.

**Advantages:** Provides rich qualitative information.

**Limitations:** Not sufficient on its own for grading.

**Associated techniques:** Systematic observation, Incidental observation, Oral exchanges, Evaluative interview

**Compatible media/evidence:** Laboratory practice, Brainstorming, Class participation, Structured oral question, Colloquium, Role play, Interview, Escape room or gamified challenge, Laboratory checklist

**Associated dimensions:** Simulation

---


### 16. Descriptive record

_Detailed record of performances, processes or behaviours._

**Type:** Qualitative record · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The descriptive record is a narrative assessment instrument in which the teacher describes in some detail the performance of a student or group during an activity or period, without reducing it to a score.

How to use it: write a qualitative description explaining what the student did, how they did it and what evidence was observed. It can be structured by criteria or by moments in the activity. It is more elaborate than the anecdotal record and more appropriate for periodic reports.

It allows families and the student themselves to receive a rich and nuanced picture of learning, beyond what a numerical grade can convey.

**Useful for:** Documenting observable evidence in detail.

**Suitable for:** Complex processes or individual monitoring.

**Advantages:** Allows the context of the evidence to be understood.

**Limitations:** Time-consuming.

**Associated techniques:** Probing and questioning

**Compatible media/evidence:** Project defence, Laboratory practice, Simulation, KPSI, Initial concept map, Brainstorming

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose, Performance evidence

### 17. Teacher's diary

_Notebook or document with observations and pedagogical decisions._

**Type:** Teacher record · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The teacher's diary is a notebook or digital document where the teacher records reflections on how lessons develop: what worked, what did not, what students learned, what difficulties arose and what would be changed next time.

How to use it: it is worth writing in it regularly (at the end of each session or week) and reflectively, not just descriptively. Questions such as 'What learning evidence did I observe today?', 'Which students concern me and why?' or 'What would I adjust?' guide the reflection.

It is not just a record of what happened, but a tool for improving teaching practice. Over time, it becomes a valuable source of information for adjusting planning.

**Useful for:** Recording monitoring, incidents and adjustments.

**Suitable for:** Continuous and formative assessment.

**Advantages:** Helps make teaching decisions.

**Limitations:** Can be unsystematic if not structured.

**Associated techniques:** Incidental observation

**Compatible media/evidence:** Class participation, Cooperative work, Laboratory practice

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose

### 18. Individual tracking sheet

_Record of a student's progress over time._

**Type:** Monitoring · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The individual tracking sheet gathers, for each student, cumulative information about their development over a period: tasks submitted, results, observations, attendance at tutorials, improvement commitments.

How to use it: design a card per student with the criteria or indicators to be monitored. The teacher updates it periodically with information from different sources. At the end of the period, it offers a longitudinal view of progress.

Especially useful for personalised monitoring in groups with diverse needs, for preparing individual tutorials and for grounding promotion decisions.

**Useful for:** Documenting development and needs.

**Suitable for:** Long processes, reinforcement, tutoring.

**Advantages:** Allows a longitudinal view.

**Limitations:** Requires constant updating.

**Associated techniques:** Probing and questioning, Analysis of processes and reflections

**Compatible media/evidence:** Digital portfolio, Project defence, Individual classroom work, Learning portfolio, Project report, Work plan, Prototype, Collaborative document, Partial submission, Project log

**Associated dimensions:** Project-based learning, Continuous assessment

### 19. Group tracking sheet

_Record of a group's progress and functioning._

**Type:** Monitoring · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher/Group

The group tracking sheet is a collective version of the individual tracking sheet: it gathers in one table information on all students in the group, making it possible to compare and detect general trends.

How to use it: each row is a student and each column is a criterion, task or moment of observation. The teacher updates it periodically. A glance at the whole reveals which students need more attention, which criteria the group in general has not reached and which aspects have improved.

It is a very useful classroom monitoring tool for teachers with multiple groups, as it maintains a global view of the state of learning.

**Useful for:** Assessing coordination, tasks and agreements.

**Suitable for:** Projects and cooperative work.

**Advantages:** Makes the team's functioning visible.

**Limitations:** Can hide individual differences if used alone.

**Compatible media/evidence:** Project defence, Cooperative work, Collaborative document

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose, Project-based learning

### 20. Participation record

_Note of contributions and quality of input._

**Type:** Record · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Low/Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The participation record documents students' contributions during sessions: who participates, how often, what quality the contributions have and whether they respect turn-taking and participation rules.

How to use it: it can be as simple as a student list where each contribution is marked, or as elaborate as an observation scale assessing the quality of contributions. The key is that it is manageable during class and does not distract the teacher from their main role.

It allows objective feedback to be given on participation, identifies who never contributes and grounds this element if it forms part of the grade.

**Useful for:** Tracking oral or digital participation.

**Suitable for:** Debates, forums, classes, assemblies.

**Advantages:** Allows participation to be made objective.

**Limitations:** Quantity must not be confused with quality.

**Compatible media/evidence:** Debate, Discussion forum, Assembly, Colloquium, Class participation, Socratic seminar

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose, Performance evidence

### 21. Cooperative work record

_Document for noting roles, contributions and group compliance._

**Type:** Record · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher/Group

The cooperative work record documents how the group functions during collaborative activities: distribution of roles, completion of individual tasks, quality of communication and conflict resolution.

How to use it: it can be combined with direct observation by the teacher, group self-assessment and peer assessment among members. Recording what happens in each group during the session allows timely intervention if a group is having difficulties.

Especially useful in extended group projects and activities, where the team's functioning carries as much weight as the final result.

**Useful for:** Assessing cooperation and responsibility.

**Suitable for:** Group work.

**Advantages:** Helps assess the cooperative process.

### 22. Field notes

_Teacher's notes during activities, practical sessions or field trips._

**Type:** Qualitative record · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Low · **Participation:** Teacher

Field notes are brief, quick and informal notes taken by the teacher during class to avoid losing learning evidence that would otherwise be forgotten. They are the raw material that can later be elaborated into more formal records.

How to use them: they can be taken on paper, a phone or a tablet. The key is that they are immediate, specific and dated. They do not need to be complete or formal; the aim is to capture the essentials for later retrieval.

They are the basis of incidental observation and anecdotal records. Over time, accumulated field notes allow the progress of each student to be seen with more precision than the teacher's memory.

**Useful for:** Collecting contextualised observations.

**Suitable for:** Field trips, laboratory, projects, fieldwork.

**Advantages:** Very flexible and quick.

**Limitations:** Require subsequent organisation.

**Associated techniques:** Incidental observation

**Compatible media/evidence:** Exit ticket, Research report, Laboratory practice, Brainstorming, Field notebook

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose

### 23. Assessment card

_Brief document for assessing a specific task._

**Type:** Assessment · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** Low/Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The assessment card is a structured document that gathers the assessment of a student or piece of work according to predefined criteria, with space for scores, levels and qualitative comments.

How to use it: design it with the criteria relevant to the task, the rating scale and space for observations. It can be given to the student after marking as a feedback document. If used for all submissions, the student can see their progress throughout the course.

More flexible than a fixed rubric and more informative than a grade alone. Suitable for periodic assessments of individual work.

**Useful for:** Recording a synthetic assessment.

**Suitable for:** Tasks of low or medium complexity.

**Advantages:** Practical and adaptable.

**Limitations:** Less complete than a rubric.

**Compatible media/evidence:** Summary, Text commentary

### 24. Feedback card

_Template for returning strengths and areas for improvement._

**Type:** Feedback · **Phase:** Process · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher/Peers

The feedback card is a document designed specifically to communicate to the student what they have done well, what they can improve and how to do it. It goes beyond a score and focuses on actionable information.

How to use it: a common structure includes: 'What have you done well?', 'What can you improve?' and 'How can you improve it?'. The 'two stars and a wish' format or a table with strengths and areas for improvement can also be used. It is given together with or instead of the grade, especially in formative assessment.

Feedback is most effective when it is specific, timely and improvement-oriented. A well-designed card helps the teacher be systematic in this process.

**Useful for:** Guiding improvement in learning.

**Suitable for:** Drafts, projects, written productions.

**Advantages:** Facilitates clear and actionable feedback.

**Limitations:** Does not always produce a grade.

**Associated techniques:** Analysis of productions, Analysis of processes and reflections

**Compatible media/evidence:** Two stars and a wish, Draft, Project defence, Partial submission, Laboratory report, Learning diary, Reflection sheet, Peer review, Exit ticket, AI process log

**Associated dimensions:** Formative purpose, Feedback and feedforward, Traceability, authorship and AI use

### 25. Assessment matrix

_Table relating criteria, indicators and levels._

**Type:** Assessment · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher

The assessment matrix is a table that crosses assessment criteria with achievement levels, similar to a rubric but often more concise. It can include qualitative descriptors, scores or both.

How to use it: design it with criteria in rows and levels in columns. Each cell can contain a description, a score or both. When assessing, mark the cell corresponding to the level observed for each criterion.

It is a generic term that encompasses analytic rubrics. The main difference from a rubric is that the matrix can be more schematic and does not always include complete descriptors in each cell.

**Useful for:** Organising complex assessment.

**Suitable for:** Tasks with multiple criteria.

**Advantages:** Structures criteria and levels.

**Limitations:** Can be complex for students.

**Compatible media/evidence:** Project defence, Laboratory report, Oral presentation, Case study

**Associated dimensions:** Competency-based approach, Summative purpose

### 26. Descriptor bank

_Collection of phrases for describing performance levels._

**Type:** Assessment support · **Phase:** Design · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The descriptor bank is a collection of descriptive phrases ready to use in constructing rubrics, reports and feedback cards. It avoids having to write descriptors from scratch each time.

How to use it: it is organised by criteria, competencies or areas, with descriptors for different performance levels. When designing a rubric, the teacher selects and adapts descriptors from the bank instead of writing them from scratch.

It is an efficiency tool that reduces the time needed to design assessment instruments and promotes terminological coherence across different subjects or teachers in the same department.

**Useful for:** Writing rubrics and reports.

### 27. Achievement profile

### 28. Assessment target

### 37. Criteria grading table

_Table relating evidence to assessment criteria._

**Type:** Grading · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher

The criteria grading table organises the final grade broken down by assessment criteria, instead of giving a single overall mark. Each criterion has its own weight and grade.

How to use it: list each assessment criterion with its weighting and the grade obtained. The final mark results from the weighted sum. This format is required by current regulations for assessment by evaluation criteria and specific competencies.

It allows students and families to know exactly which criteria the student is performing well in and which need improvement, making the grade much more informative.

### 38. Weighting matrix

_Table of relative weights of criteria, evidence or tools._

**Type:** Grading · **Phase:** Final · **Complexity:** High · **Participation:** Teacher

The weighting matrix specifies what relative weight each criterion, competency or task has in the final grade. It defines the rules of the game before the assessment process begins.

How to use it: draw up a table with all assessable elements and their corresponding percentage or weight, verifying that the sum is 100%. It should be communicated to students at the start of the period so they can organise their effort in an informed way.

A good weighting matrix reflects pedagogical priorities: if the process is as important as the product, both should have a significant weight.

**Useful for:** Calculating grades when weighting is involved.

**Suitable for:** Summative or end-of-term assessment.

**Advantages:** Makes the weight of each element explicit.

**Limitations:** Can make assessment too rigid.

### 39. Numerical scale

_Assessment expressed with numbers._

**Type:** Grading · **Phase:** Final · **Complexity:** Low · **Participation:** Teacher

The numerical scale assigns a score on a number scale (e.g., 1–10, 0–100, 1–4) to each criterion or to the work as a whole. It is the most widespread grading system in the Spanish educational system.

How to use it: each score on the scale should have a clear meaning (what a 5 implies as opposed to a 7). Without explicit criteria, the numerical scale can be subjective and inconsistent across marking sessions. Combined with qualitative descriptors, it gains in reliability.

Its advantage is ease of communication and calculation. Its limitation is that it does not inform students of what to improve, so it must always be accompanied by qualitative feedback.

**Useful for:** Communicating a quantitative grade.

**Suitable for:** Final or partial results.

**Advantages:** Compatible with administrative systems.

**Limitations:** Can hide qualitative information.

**Associated techniques:** Specific tests

### 40. Verbal scale

The verbal scale replaces numbers with qualitative descriptors: insufficient, sufficient, good, notable, excellent; or beginning, developing, achieved, outstanding. Each level describes a degree of achievement.

How to use it: associate each verbal level with a description of what it implies and, if necessary, an equivalent numerical score. It is the scale used by the curriculum of many educational stages.

It has the advantage of being more descriptive than a numerical grade and communicating achievement level better. It is the basis of competency achievement profiles.

**Useful for:** Communicating achievement level.

### 41. Level-to-grade converter

_Rule that transforms achievement levels into a grade._

**Type:** Grading · **Phase:** Final · **Complexity:** Medium · **Participation:** Teacher

The level-to-grade converter is a table or formula that translates the qualitative levels of a rubric or descriptive scale into a numerical grade, facilitating the grading process when the curriculum requires a numerical mark.

How to use it: establish the equivalence between levels and scores (e.g., 'excellent = 9–10', 'good = 7–8', 'satisfactory = 6', 'sufficient = 5', 'insufficient = 1–4'). If there are several weighted criteria, the converter calculates the final mark from the assigned levels.

It allows qualitative, learning-oriented assessment to be combined with the regulatory obligation to issue numerical grades.

**Useful for:** Converting rubric levels or achievement levels into grades.

**Suitable for:** When a final grade is needed.

**Advantages:** Facilitates consistency in grading.

**Limitations:** Must be transparent and justifiable.

**Compatible media/evidence:** Oral presentation, Project defence, Project report

### 58. AI and sources use declaration

_Template for declaring support, sources, AI tools and personal decisions in a task._

**Type:** Traceability/authorship · **Phase:** Process/Final · **Complexity:** Low/Medium · **Participation:** Student/Teacher

The AI and sources use declaration is a short template that accompanies a task to explain which external supports were used and which part of the work reflects the student's own decisions.

How to use it: it can include sections on sources consulted, AI tools used, the purpose of each support, revised fragments, errors found, changes made and final responsibility for the content. It is more useful when requested together with drafts, revision history or a short defence of the work.

It does not replace a rubric or prove learning by itself, but it helps turn AI use into a transparent, reviewable and educationally useful practice.

**Useful for:** Document authorship, AI use, source checking and responsibility for the final production.

**Suitable for:** Written productions, projects, portfolios, digital products and tasks completed outside the classroom.

**Advantages:** Makes the process transparent and supports feedback conversations about authorship and work quality.

**Limitations:** It depends on student honesty and should be combined with process review or oral defence when needed.

**Associated techniques:** Analysis of processes and reflections, Documentary analysis, Evidence triangulation
